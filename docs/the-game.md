# The game

What Vale Meridian is, how an evening plays, and why it is built the way it is. Hosts should read this first; [host-guide.md](host-guide.md) turns it into a plan. This page contains spoilers.

## Premise

*Vale Meridian is fictional and is not linked to any real company of that or a similar name (see the disclaimer in the [README](../README.md)).*

On **10 October 1986** Vale Meridian Studios, the television empire of the legendary Vincent Vale, holds a gala. Everyone in the room has a reason to be there and something they would prefer nobody knew. During the toast, Vale drinks from his private decanter and collapses.

The real party is whatever occasion you are celebrating; the fiction happens in 1986. Fictional evidence is dated 10 October 1986, so keep the two dates distinct in your head and on your props.

Tone: 1980s glamour, affectionate satire and an Agatha Christie atmosphere. Immersive, celebratory and PG-13. It is a party, not a puzzle exam: the aim is conversation, gossip, questioning and discovery, not guaranteeing that every person hears every fact.

## The people in the room

| Role | How many | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Players** | 52 | Each has a pre-party briefing, dress guidance and a secret, then four short cards over the evening |
| **Core suspects** | 17 of the 52 | Characters with the strongest motives; the red herrings and the solution live here |
| **The Game Master** | 1 | The host. A non-acting "Family Solicitor" who hands out envelopes, keeps time and gives the conclusion. Not a suspect |
| **The child cameo** | 1 | A tiny helicopter pilot with no clues and no duties |
| **Performers** | about 9 of the players | A few characters have a short acting moment (below) |

That makes 54 seats. One of the 52 players performs both **Vincent Vale** and, after the collapse, his twin **Peter Vale**, so the story has 53 named characters but 52 players.

Characters who have a part to play beyond chatting:

- **Vincent Vale / Peter Vale** gives the toast, collapses, then returns as his twin brother Peter at fictional 19:55.
- **DJ Spinny T** runs the music: one twenty-second cue at the toast, four cues in the finale.
- **Leo Lights and Wanda Wires** change the stage lighting on Spinny's cue.
- **Scooter** carries a sealed silver compact to the murderer.
- **Dakota Steele** recovers that compact after the collapse.
- **Vance Vellum** takes Polaroids all evening and hands out photograph packs.
- **Inspector Donna Reed** hands a laboratory report to every table in Round 3 and, at the end, makes the arrest.
- **The murderer** reads (or has read to them) a short closing speech, cold.

Everyone else only has to talk.

## The rounds

| Round | When | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **0: arrival** | The reception, before seating | Guests mingle. Each card names a small group of three or four to find. Vale's toast, the staged collapse and the compact are set up quietly |
| **1** | After the collapse, when everyone sits down | Envelopes are waiting at the place settings. Peter arrives. The first leads and the first secrets |
| **2** | Released together by the Game Master | Fresh cards, Vance's nine photograph packs, Dakota's envelope |
| **3** | Released together by the Game Master | Final cards. Donna puts the laboratory report on every table |
| **4: closing** | After the tables have talked | Everyone writes their own conclusion; a standing elimination through motive, means and opportunity; the case; the name; the speech; the arrest; the credits; back to the party |

Every round's cards are released together, and the cards for a round are written to stand on their own: nobody has to have spoken to anyone first.

## How the clues travel

The network is the game. Every ordinary card does three things for its player, in the character's own voice, without printing any of them as a heading:

1. gives them a fact of their own,
2. tells them why it matters now, and
3. sends them to **one named person** with a reason to talk.

The person they are sent to has the other half. The facts are spread so that no single conversation answers the case, and so that a missed conversation does not stop the evening: important facts have more than one route. The cards use character names for directions, never seat numbers, so guests can be moved around on the night.

Because 52 people cannot all walk about, a card that sends its player to someone at another table comes with a **pre-printed question note**: a short draft in the asker's voice with room to add to it and a blank reply. 59 of these are provided. Anyone can still get up and talk instead.

The Game Master does **not** supply testimony, summarise the case or collect confirmations during Rounds 1–3. The one exception is physical evidence: handing someone a laboratory report or a photograph is a practical act, and guests interpret it themselves.

## How it ends

The finale is scripted so it can be rehearsed without the murderer being known in advance. One sheet carries every spoken line, four music cues, the standing elimination, the arrest and the credits. Three people hold it (the Game Master, DJ Spinny T and Inspector Donna Reed); the Game Master's copy has the murderer's name filled in, and the other two see "[THE NAME]". Nobody is required to perform a confession: the Game Master offers to read it. The arrest involves no restraint of any kind.

The scripts are in [../story/round4-closing-sequence.md](../story/round4-closing-sequence.md) and [../story/round4-murderer-speech.md](../story/round4-murderer-speech.md). The Print Studio prints the shared and Game Master copies separately.

## What the game deliberately does not do

- It does not make guests memorise anything, take notes, keep a score or collect evidence receipts. Notes are optional; plain paper will do.
- It does not need real substances or a real mess. The compact is empty, the "laboratory findings" are a typed report, and nobody puts anything in a drink.
- It does not require an alibi for everyone. Some characters can account for an interval; nobody is asked to prove a complete alibi.
- It does not hold the room to a clock. Times in the story are fictional; the toast is not tied to a wall-clock minute.

For the principles behind this, and the rules to follow when editing a card, see [design-principles.md](design-principles.md).
